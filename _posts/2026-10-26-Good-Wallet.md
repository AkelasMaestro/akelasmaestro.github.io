This is one of those life principle things that is both sound advice and a deeper metaphor. 

## TL;DR

Have a proper wallet Something that can hold all your bank cards, library card, ID, insurance card, and a good amount of cash (If you don't have a library card, get one of those too). Make sure it's durable and looks good. You need to be prepared to handle what life might throw at you. 

Also, keep that wallet in a front pocket. It prevents back issues from sitting on an uneven lump, and pick pockets are easier to deal with. 

Also Also, keep cash on you. You figure out the amount, but make it a useful amount. Cards fail sometimes. Tips are better in cash. It's easier casually exchange than having to coordinate Venmo in a quick moment.

## Style Guide

This is more for the men. Ladies and others, you do what you want with this info, but it's not written to you.

1) Get something that will last. Cheap, fake leather does not last. You'll just add to the landfill.
2) do __not__ have Velcro on your wallet. the only people I see with Velcro closures on their wallets are  people who don't seem to have their life together. Like middle schoolers.
3) Size it properly to hold all the stuff you need. Bonus if it has a coin pouch.

A sturdy leather wallet is a classic. My Dad and I came to like [Mighty Wallets](https://mightywallet.shop/products/explorer?variant=1152000098324) which are made of Tyvek. thin, light, durable, and still able to hold plenty of cash and cards. Find what works for you. 

Thus concludes the light hearted portion of this article. I warn you now, it gets a little heavy and a little preachy. 

## Story Time

For a couple years, I was fixated on finding the smallest wallet. This was an extension of my general desire to optimize and simplify my life wherever possible. After several iterations, I ended up settling on a small binder clip. Cheaper and surprisingly more effective than a money clip, it was perfect at holding my bus pass, my driver's license, my debit card, and a little bit of cash. Cheap, effective tools just tickle my brain and even now I think back on the binder clip fondly. 

And then my dad died[^1]. Suddenly I found myself planning a funeral and coordinating the family. I was receiving cash donations from family members and then turning around to pass it on to whoever I was sending on errands. A year and half later, I'm no longer in that rapid-fire chaos, but my responsibilities have increased. My wife and I are more established financially; we have both shared and individual checking accounts as well as a credit card. I am trustee over my mother's finances and often help her with my little sisters and whatever projects she has going on, so that's a couple more checking accounts and their associated cards. My little binder clip is just not up to the task. 

During the initial fall out of my father's passing, I borrowed his wallet. At first, it was to manage all the cash I suddenly had to deal with, but it became more than that. Dad's wallet, and everything it contained, was extension of how he provided and protected the family. Even now, in death, he continues to give through his life insurance and social security benefits. The family is housed, fed, clothed, and hopeful for the future thanks to what he continues to give us. Picking up his wallet felt like picking up a mantle,  of picking up responsibility. If a wallet is supposed to hold all of that, make sure it is a good one.

## The Moral

Some of the best stories are the ones we leave up for interpretation. Take what you will from my story, I think my point is obvious enough. There are other related themes I want to record here.

First, anyone that tells you money does not buy happiness either doesn't know how to spend it or is trying to pacify those without it. When my grandpa, we call him Papa, came to help with the funeral, he was beside himself worrying how my mom would pay the mortgage and keep the lights on. He about collapsed when I told him Dad had a life insurance policy specifically for that purpose. Later, I found myself on the brink of tears when Papa offered to handle all the funeral costs as I struggled to get access to Dad's finances. Money is a powerful tool that opens doors and it is best spent on others.

Second, A man without charity is nothing. That was one of my dad's favorite scriptures. Look for ways  to give to others. Plan and prepare for it. In discussions of charity, it is common to talk of all the non-monetary methods we can give to each other in time, talents, and attention, but money talks. If you only give when it is convenient to you (I will let you define what convenient means), you can give more.

Another story. My in-laws live in Brazil and their washing machine was falling apart. Replacing it would cost [three weeks wages](2026-11-02-International-Exchange-Rates.md#). My father-in-law kept every tool and piece of scrap he came across to keep the machine, and the rest of the house, ticking along. Due to the 5:1 exchange rate, replacing the machine wasn't the same kind of hardship for my wife and I. Nor was spending a similar amount on a new set of tools for my father-in-law. "See a need, fill a need"[^2]. Seeing the relief and joy on my in-laws' faces when they received their respective gifts is a special memory I hold dear. 

Third, be prepared. Do not optimize your life such that the smallest disturbance pushes you over. It is a pain when a late night is made even later by the need to go home to get the right card before going to the store. It is a small joy to have the requisite quarters needed to buy a bouncy ball out of those machines for your little sister. It is a large joy to get someone out of a nasty pickle. 

Take the time to know people. Watch what they need. Do what you can make their lives a little better. As you practice, you will get better over time. Money is is only good if it's spent, (it can also be spent on the future i.e. retirement, savings, and life insurance). Let it flow and enjoy it with others.
